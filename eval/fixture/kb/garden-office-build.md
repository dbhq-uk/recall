# Garden Office Build

Log of building a garden office at the bottom of the garden, started May 2023, first day of proper use as a workspace was 2 September 2023.

## Planning

### Permitted development

Checked the permitted development rules first. Outbuildings are allowed without planning permission provided they're single-storey, sit behind the rear wall of the house, and — the rule that actually constrained the design — stay under 2.5m in overall height if built within 2m of a boundary. Our plot is narrow enough that the office sits close to the fence, so the design was kept to a 2.4m eaves height with a shallow pitched roof to stay under the limit.

### Size and siting

Footprint settled on 3m x 2.4m, oriented with the long wall facing the house for the best light through the double doors. Sits on what used to be an overgrown gravel path, roughly 8m from the back door.

## Foundation

### Deck blocks

Used nine GroundWorx concrete deck blocks rather than a full slab or screw piles — cheaper, faster, no digger needed given well-draining soil once the old gravel path was dug out. Blocks set on a 75mm compacted hardcore bed, checked level and packed with sharp sand where needed, three rows of three, spaced to land under the joist positions.

## Frame

### Floor and walls

Floor built from 47x150mm C24 timber joists at 400mm centres, sat on the deck blocks, with 18mm WBP plywood decking on top. Wall studwork is 47x100mm CLS timber, also at 400mm centres, built as separate wall panels on the workshop floor (well, the garage floor) and then lifted into place and screwed together at the corners — much easier than trying to stick-build the walls in situ.

### Insulation

Celotex GA4000 rigid foam board, 100mm thickness, cut to fit snugly between the wall studs with minimal gaps, taped at every joint with foil tape to maintain the vapour barrier. Roof insulation is a thinner 25mm Celotex board above the rafters, under the roofing deck, since the shallow pitch didn't leave room for anything thicker without pushing the height over the permitted development limit.

## Roofing

EPDM rubber membrane (Firestone RubberCover), single sheet with no seams across the whole 3m x 2.4m roof deck, glued down with the manufacturer's contact adhesive and dressed over a timber drip edge trim on all four sides. Chose EPDM over felt shingles mainly for the no-seams, 20-plus-year lifespan — felt would have been cheaper but I didn't want to be back up there re-felting within a decade.

## Cladding

Featheredge larch boards, untreated (larch is naturally rot-resistant, though it will silver over time), fixed horizontally with a 20mm overlap between boards, stainless steel ring-shank nails to avoid rust streaking down the timber.

## Electrics

Ran power from the house consumer unit as a dedicated circuit, buried in a duct at 600mm depth across the lawn — deeper than the 450mm minimum just for extra peace of mind given the number of times that stretch of lawn gets a fork put through it for aerating.

```text
# garden office electrics - circuit spec
cable:      SWA (steel wire armoured), 2.5mm, 3-core
protection: 20A MCB + 30mA RCD at the house consumer unit
run:        approx 11m, buried duct, 600mm depth
termination: small consumer unit in the office, 2-way
```

Inside, that feeds a small 2-way consumer unit: one circuit for sockets, one for lighting. Cable termination and the connection back at the house consumer unit was done by a qualified electrician (Part P notifiable work); everything else — cladding, insulation, first-fix stud positions — done myself.

## Windows and doors

Aluminium-framed double doors and a single fixed window, both double-glazed, from a local fabricator rather than a big DIY chain — quicker turnaround and the frame colour (anthracite grey, RAL 7016) matched the house windows without an upcharge for a "non-standard" colour.

## Build log highlights

The trickiest single day was getting the wall panels upright and square without a second pair of hands — my usual helper was away that weekend, so it meant a lot of temporary diagonal bracing screwed into the floor deck, checking each panel plumb with a spirit level, adjusting, and repeating for all four walls before screwing the corners together. A two-hour job with two people took most of a Saturday alone.

Roofing was rushed slightly — the EPDM went down under time pressure with rain forecast two days out, and the adhesive could have done with another hour of cure time before the drip trim edges went on. No leaks so far, but it's a reminder that weather deadlines and adhesive cure times don't always cooperate.

Total build, weekends only, ran from early May to end of August 2023 — about four months, though hands-on-tools time was probably closer to five or six weekends once foundation, frame, insulation, cladding and roofing are all added up.
