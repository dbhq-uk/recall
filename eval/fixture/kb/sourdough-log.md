# Sourdough Log

Kept since the starter (named Gary, after the house's previous owner, an apparently keen baker) arrived from a neighbour in January 2023.

## The starter

### Feeding schedule

Gary lives on the kitchen windowsill, fed 1:1:1 (starter:flour:water by weight) once a day when active, twice daily in warm weather. Dark rye flour only for feeding — never switched to white, keeps him more vigorous.

### Flour stock

Two flours in rotation for the loaves themselves:

- **Shipton Mill Organic Strong White Bread Flour No. 4** — workhorse, good gluten development, reliable spring.
- **Marriage's Malthouse Blend** — used one bake in four, adds malty sweetness and denser crumb, good with soup.

## Standard loaf method

The recipe I've settled on after roughly eighteen months of tinkering, written out so I stop reconstructing it from memory every Saturday morning.

Total dough hydration sits at 78%, which for Shipton Mill No. 4 gives a fairly open crumb without being unmanageable. Ratios for a single 900g loaf:

```text
# standard loaf - single batch
500g   strong white flour (Shipton Mill No. 4)
390g   water (78% hydration)
100g   active starter (Gary, fed 4-6 hours prior)
10g    fine sea salt
```

Mix flour and water first, autolyse one hour covered. Add starter and salt, mix to combine, then four stretch-and-folds at 30-minute intervals. Bulk ferment continues after the last fold — roughly 5 hours at 24°C, looking for about 50% volume increase and visible surface bubbles rather than clock-watching.

Shape into a tight boule, into a well-floured banneton, then straight into the fridge for a cold retard of 12-16 hours. Baking cold, straight from the fridge, gives better oven spring than letting it come to room temperature first — counterintuitive but it's held up every time tested side by side.

## Baking

Oven preheated to 250°C fan with the Lodge combination cooker (shallow pan as lid, deep pan as base) inside for at least 45 minutes. Score the loaf, load into the hot base, lid on, into the oven.

- 20 minutes at 250°C, lid on
- Lid off, drop to 230°C fan
- 20-25 minutes further, until deep amber-brown, internal temp 96-98°C on the probe thermometer

## Troubleshooting: the great starter crisis of August 2023

Went away for a fortnight in August 2023 and left Gary in the fridge, unfed, normally fine for up to three weeks. Came back to a starter separated into a grey liquid layer (hooch) over a dense, sludgy paste — not unusual on its own — but after two days of normal feeding it still wasn't rising, and the smell had gone from pleasantly sour to something closer to nail varnish remover.

Rather than binning it, tried a rescue: discarded down to 20g of the original culture, fed with equal parts rye flour and water at a much smaller ratio (20:20:20) to reduce the load on whatever yeast remained, moved to a warmer spot near the boiler (around 24°C rather than the usual 19°C windowsill). First 24 hours showed no activity, worrying. Second feed, same ratio, and by the following morning a visible doubling within about six hours — a good sign the wild yeast had outcompeted whatever bacterial overgrowth had taken hold.

Kept up twice-daily small feeds for four days before trusting it enough to bake with again. First loaf back was noticeably denser than usual, but by the second bake it was back to normal oven spring. Lesson recorded so I don't panic next time: don't discard a sluggish starter after a long fridge stint, just cut the ratio right down and feed more frequently before giving up on it.

Looking back, a few things probably compounded the problem beyond the fortnight itself. The jar had been sealed with the lid fully tightened rather than left loose, so whatever gas built up during the first week away had nowhere to go — on previous, shorter fridge stints the lid was always left just resting on top, and those never produced anything close to that smell. There's also a chance the fridge ran slightly warmer than usual that fortnight; it's an old integrated model with no digital readout, and a neighbour watering the plants mentioned the kitchen felt unusually warm, pointing at a thermostat or door-seal issue rather than the starter being fragile. A friend who's kept a starter for a decade reckons the nail-varnish smell is almost always excess acetic acid from bacteria outcompeting the yeast in low-oxygen, cold conditions — lines up with the lid-sealed theory well enough to write down: leave the lid loose, and get a cheap fridge thermometer rather than trust the dial.

## Other bakes

Tried a rye-heavy loaf (60% dark rye, 40% white, 85% hydration) in October 2023 — stickier dough, needed a loaf tin rather than free-standing shaping, longer bake at a lower temperature (220°C, 45 minutes) to avoid a burnt crust before the dense crumb cooked through. Worth repeating with more practice at that hydration.
