# Cooking Technique Notes

A collection of methods I keep having to relearn or look up, written down properly this time.

## Meat

### Reverse sear steak

The single biggest upgrade to steak night since I started doing this in 2022. Works best on thick cuts — anything under 3cm is too thin for the method to really pay off.

1. Season generously with salt at least 40 minutes before cooking (dry brine — draws moisture out then reabsorbs it with the salt, better crust later)
2. Oven at 120°C, cook on a wire rack over a tray until the internal temperature hits 48-50°C for medium-rare (probe thermometer, not guesswork)
3. Rest 5 minutes
4. Blistering hot cast iron pan, a minute or so each side just to build a crust, basting with butter, thyme and a crushed garlic clove in the last 30 seconds

Compared to a straight pan-sear from raw, this gives a much more even edge-to-edge colour with almost no grey overcooked band beneath the crust. Downside is it takes the best part of an hour with the oven stage, so it's not a weeknight method.

### Stock

Chicken stock, from carcasses saved in the freezer after roast dinners rather than bought specially. Roasting the bones first (200°C, 25 minutes, until properly browned) before they go in the pot makes a noticeably deeper, less watery stock than starting from raw bones — worth the extra step every time.

Simmer, never boil, for about 4 hours, topping up water as needed, with a rough mirepoix (onion, carrot, celery) added after the first hour rather than at the start, since long-simmered vegetables mostly just turn to mush and give little back in flavour by comparison to fresher additions partway through.

## Bread and dough

### Pizza dough

00 flour (Caputo, the red "Pizzeria" bag) is worth the extra cost over standard strong white for this specifically — much higher gluten extensibility, stretches thin without tearing or springing back the way ordinary bread flour does. 65% hydration, 3% salt, tiny amount of dried yeast (2g per 500g flour), cold-proved in the fridge for 24-48 hours in individual dough balls rather than one bulk mass, which makes the final shaping far more manageable.

Oven can't get anywhere near a real pizza oven's heat, so the workaround is a pizza steel preheated for a full hour at the oven's maximum (usually 250-280°C depending on the oven), finished for the last minute or two under the grill element to colour the top before the base burns underneath.

## Fermented vegetables

### Sauerkraut

White cabbage, shredded fine, 2% salt by weight of the cabbage, massaged and packed tightly into a jar to submerge under its own brine within a few hours. Left at room temperature for 2-3 weeks, tasting from about day 10 onwards to catch the point of sourness preferred — I like it on the milder side, around day 12-14, rather than the sharper, longer-fermented versions some people prefer.

### Kimchi

Similar principle but with napa cabbage, a paste of gochugaru, garlic, ginger, fish sauce and a little sugar rubbed through the leaves after an initial salt-brine soak and rinse. Ferments faster than sauerkraut, usually good within 5-7 days at room temperature, then moved to the fridge to slow things down once it's at the preferred sourness.

## Knife sharpening

Bought a whetstone set (King combination stone, 1000/6000 grit) after years of a pull-through sharpener leaving the edge worse, not better, over time. Angle held at roughly 17 degrees, checked with a cheap plastic angle guide clipped to the spine of the blade until the feel of it became familiar enough to do freehand.

Rough method: soak the 1000 grit side for 10 minutes before use, work the edge until a burr is felt along the whole length on the opposite side, flip and repeat, then move to the 6000 grit side for a much lighter polishing pass to refine the edge rather than reshape it. Strop on a leather belt afterwards. Takes maybe 15 minutes per knife once the coarse reshaping stage isn't needed (which is most routine sharpenings — the heavy 1000-grit work is really only for a genuinely blunt or damaged edge).

## Short note: resting meat

Always undercounted how much residual cooking happens during rest — a steak pulled at 50°C internal will often drift up another 2-3°C while resting, which matters when chasing a specific doneness.
