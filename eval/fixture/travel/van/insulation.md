# Insulation

## Why insulation matters

A steel panel van is basically a giant heatsink with a person living inside it. Get the insulation wrong and you get two problems at once: it's miserable to sleep in below about 5°C, and condensation runs down the inside of the panels and pools under the floor where you can't see it until the ply starts to delaminate. Sorting the thermal bridging properly mattered more to me than the headline R-value of the bulk insulation itself.

## Materials used

### Wall and ceiling

Went with Thinsulate SM600L, 25mm, over the more common Kingspan/Celotex PIR board approach. Reasoning: Thinsulate is non-hygroscopic (doesn't hold water even if damp gets to it), it drapes into the compound curves of the roof without leaving gaps, and there's no off-gassing smell in a small enclosed space. Downside is it's not as high an R-value per millimetre as PIR, but with a van this size that wasn't the limiting factor. Bought 15m² on a roll from Vanlife Outfitters, £145 delivered.

### Thermal bridging on the ribs

This is the bit most first-time converters skip and regret. Every steel rib in the van body is a direct path for heat straight through to the outside, insulation or not. Ran 10mm Class O Armaflex strips directly onto every rib before the Thinsulate went over the top, glued with Armaflex 520 contact adhesive, seams taped with Armaflex HT625 aluminium foil tape. Where two ribs met at odd angles near the rear pillars I doubled up to 20mm total thickness.

### Floor

12mm Recticel Eurowall PIR board sits under an 18mm WBP birch ply subfloor. Glued down with Gorilla contact adhesive and screwed at roughly 300mm centres into the original floor ridges so the ply can't creep over time. This build-up loses about 30mm of internal height but it was worth it — the floor was the coldest surface by a clear margin on the thermal camera before we did this.

## Wheel arches

Silent Coat butyl sound-deadening panels went onto the bare metal first, then a closed-cell foam wrap over the top before boxing them in with ply. The butyl does double duty here — it cuts road roar and stops condensation forming directly on the cold metal where you'd never spot it until the smell gave it away.

## Ventilation note

Two low-level Multi-Mesh weatherproof cowl vents fitted near the floor for cross-ventilation when parked up with doors shut. Cheap insurance against damp air just sitting stagnant overnight.

## Conversion day walkthrough

Did the bulk of the insulation over two days, 18–19 June 2022, before any of the framing went in — much easier to work in an empty shell than around half-built furniture. Day one was entirely ribs and bridging: degreasing every panel first with a panel wipe, because the factory metal came with a light oil film that the contact adhesive just wouldn't grip. First attempt on the offside sliding door panel peeled straight off after twenty minutes and had to be stripped back and redone properly. Went through six cans of Thermobond spray adhesive across the whole van, more than the guide figures had suggested, mostly because the compound curves in the roof ate through it fast and a lot ended up on masking paper rather than the panel.

Day two was Thinsulate. Cut oversized and packed in tight rather than trimmed flush, since gaps are worse for performance than a bit of compression. Left the wiring conduit runs exposed rather than insulating straight over them so the cable routes stayed accessible — small thing at the time but it saved a lot of grief later when adding the extra USB circuit in the kitchen unit.

Ran a condensation check about a month later, mid-July 2022, using a cheap infrared thermometer on a cold morning. Found one damp patch behind the rear nearside wheel arch trim, traced back to a section of rib bridging I'd missed where the arch box meets the main body — the join isn't visible once the box is fitted, so it's an easy spot to skip. Pulled the trim, added an extra strip of 10mm Armaflex over the gap, re-taped the seams, and refitted. No repeat of the damp patch since. Worth doing this infrared check on any van conversion — the bridging gaps are never quite where you expect them to be, and they're invisible again the moment the lining goes back on.

Left the door cards themselves largely untouched beyond a thin foam layer, since the factory cavities are awkward to access — diminishing returns for the extra day it would have taken. Total materials spend across the insulation phase, adhesive and Thinsulate and Armaflex and floor board included, came to just under £310 — a small fraction of the overall conversion budget but the part that mattered most for how comfortable the van is to sleep in through a Scottish autumn.
